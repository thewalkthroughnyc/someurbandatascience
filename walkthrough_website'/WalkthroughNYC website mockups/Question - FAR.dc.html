What does FAR actually do to a block?

60 Cedar Street, Downtown Brooklyn.

The anchor site

60 Cedar Street sits on a standard Downtown Brooklyn block, zoned R6 general district. Next door, a 2-story rowhouse sits on the same zoning designation. Same rules, two very different buildings.

I measured both lots on site: frontage, depth, and stories. That's the raw material for the math below.

The math
FAR = total floor area ÷ lot area
2-story rowhouse
2,000 sq ft ÷ 2,000 sq ft lot
FAR 1.0
19-story tower
34,000 sq ft ÷ 8,000 sq ft lot
FAR 4.3

R6 general district allows a FAR up to roughly 3.0 as-of-right, higher with an inclusionary housing bonus — which is how a lot this size clears 4.3.

The essay

60 Cedar Street is nineteen stories. Its next-door neighbor is two. Both sit on the same R6 zoning designation, on lots of comparable size, on the same block.

Zoning didn't produce the tower. It permitted it. The rowhouse owner built to code decades ago. The tower's owner assembled a larger lot, added an affordable-housing bonus, and built to the maximum the math allowed. The zoning stayed the same. Only the math changed.

Floor area ratio is the number that explains the gap.
